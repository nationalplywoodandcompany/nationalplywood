# Thirty-Five Minutes | Why the Broad Peak Rescue Was Always Too Late

**Date:** 2026-08-18
**Target runtime:** ~19 minutes (2,9xx words at ~150 wpm)
**Audience:** USA / international, English
**Tone:** confident, fast, fact-driven documentary narration
**Structure:** hook + promise (0:00–0:15) → 7 chapters → open loop

**Angle (ours, not the source's):** Every account of this disaster asks who was late. The better question is whether anyone could have been early. Avalanche physiology says the outcome was fixed inside half an hour — which moves the real failure from the rescue to the decision to walk onto that slope at all.

---

## HOOK

At half past nine on the morning of July the thirtieth, ten climbers were alive, roped up, and moving well across a snowfield on the twelfth-highest mountain on Earth.

Thirty-five minutes later, the numbers say most of them were already dead.

The first rescue helicopter lifted off twenty-two and a half hours after that.

Almost every account of the Broad Peak disaster has fixed on that gap — the missing day, the message that never got sent, the search that started far too late. And the anger is justified. But it is also aimed at the wrong target. Over the next twenty minutes I am going to walk you through what the tracking data, the weather record and the avalanche science actually show. And what they show is harder to sit with than a slow rescue: by the time anyone in the world could have known those ten people needed help, the mountain had already finished with them. The failure happened before the snow moved. Let me show you where.

## CHAPTER ONE — THE ARITHMETIC OF A RECORD

Start with a fact that reframes everything: Nirmal Purja was not supposed to be on Broad Peak.

He had flown into Pakistan that summer for a different mountain — Gasherbrum II — and he climbed it, successfully, on the twenty-first of July. That should have been the end of his season. Objective met, permit closed, flight home.

Then he did what competitive people do. He looked at his own ledger.

Purja had spent years accumulating the fourteen peaks on Earth that rise above eight thousand metres. Not once — twice. By that July he had twelve of the fourteen banked a second time. Two remained: Broad Peak and Cho Oyu. And Broad Peak happened to be roughly forty kilometres from where he was already standing, in the same range, in the same season, with a permit system that made adding it almost trivial.

No human being had ever climbed all fourteen twice. He was two mountains away from a first that could not be taken from him.

So he changed his plans. He joined climbers from Elite Exped — the company he had co-founded — and from other outfits, walked up the Baltoro, and reached Broad Peak Base Camp.

Here is the detail that should stop you. Broad Peak is routinely described, including in Elite Exped's own marketing, as one of the more forgiving of the eight-thousanders. It is the one that people attempt as a warm-up for K2, which stands a few kilometres away. Purja had already climbed it once, in 2019, during the six-month sprint that made him famous. On his personal scale of difficulty, this was not a serious mountain. It was a box to tick on the way to something historic.

That is the first thing worth holding onto. He did not die reaching beyond his limit. He died on a detour — a mountain added late, for arithmetic, on a schedule that already had its trophy.

## CHAPTER TWO — THE FIRST CLEAR MORNING

Now the weather, because the weather is the whole story.

The Karakoram had a bad July. According to reporting by National Geographic, a violent windstorm carrying unusually high humidity hit the range around the eighteenth. Then, from roughly the twenty-second through the twenty-sixth, it snowed — heavily, for days, across the whole massif.

Mountaineers read a forecast the way a surgeon reads a chart, and plenty of them did not like what they saw. Teams across the Karakoram cancelled. National Geographic reported that two climbers on Broad Peak, David Klein and Ian Overton, looked at the outlook on the twenty-fifth, decided the mountain was not going to give them a window worth the risk, and went down.

Purja's group stayed. They waited for the weather to break.

And on the thirtieth, it broke beautifully. Clear sky. Still air. Sun on the snow. The kind of morning that shows up in the drone footage and the Instagram posts, the kind that looks, to anyone who has not studied this, like the safest possible day to climb.

Here is your first genuinely counter-intuitive fact, and it is the one that kills people every single winter, on every continent that has mountains: **the first clear day after a heavy storm is statistically among the most dangerous days to be on a slope.** Not the day of the storm. The day after.

The reason is mechanical. Five days of snowfall lay a thick new layer on top of whatever surface already existed — old sun-crusted snow, wind-polished ice, buried weak grains. Then the wind picks that new snow up, carries it across ridgelines, and packs it down on the lee sides into a dense, cohesive plate. Alpine scientists call it a wind slab. It is heavy, it is stiff, and it is sitting on a weak layer it has not bonded to. It is a loaded trigger.

Investigators studying the Broad Peak avalanche have pointed to exactly that: a slab release on wind-loaded snow over steep ground, following a stretch of storms and elevated humidity in the weeks before.

The mountain did not look dangerous that morning. That is precisely what a loaded slope looks like. Blue sky is not evidence of a stable snowpack; it is evidence of good visibility. And the whole camp had been waiting five days for permission to go.

So on the thirtieth, climbers from four separate companies moved onto the same route at more or less the same hour. Not because anyone was reckless — because the storm had rationed the mountain down to a single good morning, and every schedule, every permit and every paying client on that glacier pointed at the same square of snow.

What happened next took less than a minute.

## CHAPTER THREE — THIRTY-FIVE MINUTES

They left Camp Two that morning heading for Camp Three, planning a summit push around the sixteenth of August. The route crosses a broad snowfield before it reaches rock. Their GPS trackers put them in the middle of that crossing, at around six thousand six hundred metres, at approximately half past nine, Pakistan time.

Then the slope let go.

The trackers tell the rest with the flatness of machinery. Position after position drops down the mountain. Purja's device came to rest near five thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight metres — nearly eight hundred vertical metres below where he had been standing. Some accounts describe the debris running as much as a vertical mile down the main gully.

People at Base Camp watched it happen. A Pakistani operator, Karakoram Vision, had published a photograph of climbers strung out across that snowfield minutes earlier, and then published footage of the avalanche itself.

Now the part almost nobody covering this story has put on screen.

Avalanche survival has been studied for three decades, mostly out of Switzerland, and the curve is remarkably consistent. A completely buried victim has roughly a **ninety-three percent** chance of being alive fifteen minutes after burial. Between fifteen and thirty-five minutes, that line falls off a cliff — down to around **thirty percent**.

That collapse is not from cold. Around two-thirds of avalanche deaths are asphyxia, and they happen inside the first thirty-five minutes. Snow gets driven into the airway. The victim rebreathes their own exhaled air in a sealed pocket. Worse, warm breath melts the snow at the face and it refreezes into a smooth lid of ice — rescuers call it an ice mask — and it seals off the last of the air. Beyond that half-hour mark, survival essentially requires two things at once: a clear airway and a genuine air pocket in front of the face. Hypothermia — the thing everyone assumes is the killer — only matters for the few who get that far.

Set that against Broad Peak. Ten people, buried in the debris of a slab release at six thousand six hundred metres, in terrain so steep that trained mountaineers could not safely reach parts of it days afterwards.

The window in which any intervention on Earth could have changed the outcome was about half an hour wide, and it belonged entirely to the people on that slope. Nobody was coming from Base Camp in thirty-five minutes. Nobody was coming from anywhere.

## CHAPTER FOUR — THE CEILING AT SIX THOUSAND METRES

Which brings us to the system that was supposed to save them, and the number written into it.

Base Camp knew almost immediately. The coordinator lost contact with the team shortly after nine. Others there saw the avalanche come down.

And yet, by the account in a press release from the Baltistan Commissioner's office, Pakistani authorities were not informed until eight o'clock that night. Naila Kiani — a Pakistani mountaineer and a spokesperson for the Alpine Club of Pakistan — said her organisation learned of it at ten. She has been publicly, painfully blunt about what that cost: with earlier word, a helicopter could have flown while there was still daylight. Instead the first aircraft lifted off around eight the following morning, roughly twenty-two and a half hours after the slope released.

Ten to thirteen hours of silence, from a camp full of professionals with satellite equipment. It deserves the investigation it is getting.

But now the fact that reframes the outrage — and this is the one I would put on the thumbnail.

Pakistan requires climbing and trekking teams to carry emergency helicopter evacuation insurance, arranged through Askari Aviation, the operator authorised to run these missions and itself an arm of the Pakistan Army. That mandatory cover is written for high-altitude search and rescue **up to six thousand metres**.

The climbers were caught at roughly six thousand six hundred.

They were, in the most literal administrative sense, above the ceiling of the rescue system they were legally obliged to buy into. And the physical limit is lower still: Askari's helicopters operate to a maximum safe landing altitude in the region of five thousand metres — around a vertical kilometre below the accident site. Add thin air, unstable weather and a slope loaded enough to slide again, and the aircraft's role was never rescue. It was transport: helicopters carried teams and equipment as high as they could, and human beings walked the rest.

So there are two failures here, and they are not the same size. The communications gap is a scandal, and it should end careers. But even a flawless report at 9:35 in the morning would have summoned an aircraft that could not legally or physically reach them, to a slope where the survival curve had already run out. The thirteen hours cost hope. They did not, in all likelihood, cost lives.

## CHAPTER FIVE — THE WATCH THAT MOVED

There is one moment in this story that people could not let go of, and it is worth understanding properly, because it is a small masterpiece of how technology can lie to us.

Mingma David Sherpa — Purja's climbing partner, co-founder of Elite Exped, and an experienced high-altitude rescuer — was preparing to fly from Nepal to Pakistan to join the search. Before boarding, he shared something extraordinary: Purja's GPS watch had registered movement. Around a quarter past six in the morning, roughly twenty-two hours after the avalanche. To Mingma David it read as a man alive under the snow, conscious, waiting.

That post went around the world. For most of a day, an enormous number of people believed Nims Purja was going to walk off Broad Peak.

Then the data got read more carefully. As National Geographic reported, the device recorded a change in position while showing a speed of zero.

There are three things that make a buried tracker report movement. The person moves. The signal drifts — routine in steep terrain and worse when the antenna is under snow. Or the snow itself moves: an avalanche deposit is not a solid object, it settles and creeps for hours and days after it stops, and it carries whatever is inside it.

A speed of zero points hard at the last two. The watch was almost certainly not describing a man. It was describing a mountain still shifting its weight.

That is the cruelty of the modern version of this disaster. In 1975 a family would have waited weeks for a runner and a telegram. In 2026 they can watch a dot on a map, in something close to real time, and read hope into a rounding error.

## CHAPTER SIX — TEN NAMES, FOUR COMPANIES, ONE ROPE

We should say plainly who was on that snowfield, because the coverage has largely been about one man, and ten people died.

Six were Nepali. **Pur Bahadur Gurung**, known as Yukta, the first person from the Gurung community to summit Everest ten times, and two peaks away from completing all fourteen eight-thousanders himself. **Kili Pemba Sherpa**, who had already completed all fourteen. **Gyalzen Sherpa** — Gyalu — who held a Guinness World Record for climbing Kangchenjunga three times in a single season. **Nima Sherpa**, who had taken four eight-thousanders in one season. **Nawang Thindu Sherpa**, an Everest, Manaslu and Kangchenjunga veteran. And Purja himself.

**Sohail Sakhi** was Pakistani — a guide and photographer who had climbed K2, Kangchenjunga and Nanga Parbat, some of it without supplementary oxygen. He had told people he intended to retire from expedition work after this climb.

**Nadhira Al Harthy** was Omani, the first woman from her country to stand on Everest, a veteran of K2 and Manaslu, working toward all fourteen. She was on the mountain with a different operator entirely — Seven Summit Treks.

**Wang Zhong** was Chinese, with twelve of the fourteen behind him, six of them taken in 2026 alone.

And **Mallory Geis** was American — thirty-nine, from San Antonio, Texas, who worked in her family's real-estate business. This was her first eight-thousander. Earlier that same year she had closed her Pilates studio, telling her followers she was taking a giant leap of faith into the unknown to see where life would go.

Look at what that list actually shows, because the phrase "Nims Purja's team" hides it. These people were not one expedition. They were clients and guides from at least four different companies, on the same fixed lines, on the same morning.

That is what an eight-thousander is now. Not a team on a mountain — a queue. And an avalanche does not distinguish between the man with fifty-plus high-altitude summits and the woman on her first. It took a Guinness record holder and a first-timer in the same three seconds.

Seven bodies came down. Purja, Wang Zhong, Nima Sherpa and Kili Pemba Sherpa were located at around five thousand seven hundred metres on the second of August; the recovered were flown out to Skardu by the fifth. Three were never brought home: Sohail Sakhi, Nawang Thindu Sherpa, and Mallory Geis. With the Karakoram season closing and conditions deteriorating, they are, for the foreseeable future, still on Broad Peak.

## CHAPTER SEVEN — THE CONTESTED MAN

A word about Purja himself, and I am going to give you the whole picture rather than the tidy one.

He was born into the Magar community in Nepal, and became the first Gurkha soldier admitted to Britain's Special Boat Service — one of the most demanding selection processes in the world. He came to serious mountaineering late, in his late twenties. In 2019, he climbed all fourteen eight-thousanders in six months and six days. The record he broke was measured in years — nearly eight of them. It was not an improvement. It was a different category of claim, and a great many experienced climbers flatly did not believe it until it was done.

Netflix turned that season into *14 Peaks: Nothing Is Impossible*, and he became the most recognisable mountaineer alive.

He was also, by the end, a genuinely contested figure, and his obituaries said so. In 2024 two female climbers accused him of sexual harassment and assault, in reporting carried by the New York Times. He denied the allegations. Several sponsors stepped away without much public explanation. That same year he circulated a video alleging that rival Sherpas had deliberately cut fixed ropes high on Everest — a claim that split the community and triggered a government investigation, with Nepal's Department of Tourism examining Elite Exped over an unauthorised helicopter flight to Camp Two and training conducted without permission. A separate case, over a 2022 oxygen-cylinder explosion at a property linked to his company that killed people and destroyed the house, ended with a court ordering compensation. And purists argued for years that his oxygen-assisted, helicopter-supported, heavily commercial style was hollowing out the thing it celebrated.

Both things are true at once, and the second is the reason the first matters. Purja did more than anyone alive to convince ordinary people that the eight-thousanders were reachable — that with money, guides, fixed lines and bottled oxygen, an eight-thousand-metre summit was a thing a person with a job and a savings account could attempt.

He was right. That is exactly what it has become. And on the morning of the thirtieth of July, that world he did so much to build put a Texan on her first eight-thousander, an Omani pioneer, a retiring Pakistani guide and six Nepali professionals onto the same loaded slope on the same clear morning — and then killed all of them, including him, in the time it takes to read this sentence.

## CLOSE — OPEN LOOP

So: was the rescue too slow? Yes. Should thirteen hours of silence from a base camp full of satellite phones be investigated until somebody answers for it? Absolutely.

But if you want the lesson that actually saves the next ten people, it is not in the helicopter schedule. It is in a five-day storm, a wind-loaded slab, a queue of four companies waiting on the same weather window, and a beautiful blue morning that everyone read as an invitation.

The mountain never sent an invitation. It sent a bill, and the deadline was thirty-five minutes long.

One more thing, and it is the reason I am making the next video. Everything I have described — the humidity spike, the five-day dump, the wind loading — is not a freak sequence. Researchers working across the Himalaya and Karakoram are documenting a shift in how these ranges store and shed snow as the climate warms, and they are warning that the avalanche risk on routes climbed safely for fifty years is changing underneath the people climbing them.

Next time, I am going to show you that data: what is actually happening to the snowpack above six thousand metres, why the old rules of thumb are quietly expiring, and which of the world's most famous routes the scientists are now most worried about.
