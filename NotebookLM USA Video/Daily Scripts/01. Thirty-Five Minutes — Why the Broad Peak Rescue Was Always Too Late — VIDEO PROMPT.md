## PART 2 — STEERING PROMPT

```

NARRATOR: The same confident, fast, fact-driven documentary voice. This is the analytical core of the film — the tone should be forensic and unsentimental, building toward one uncomfortable conclusion.

OPEN: A self-contained hook in the first 15 seconds — at roughly 6,600 metres on the morning of 30 July, GPS trackers recorded ten people crossing a snowfield, and then recorded them falling. Promise the viewer the number that explains why the rescue never mattered.

COVER, IN THIS ORDER:
1. The avalanche: departure from Camp 2 toward Camp 3, a planned summit push around 16 August, trackers placing them mid-crossing at about 6,600 m at approximately 9:30 a.m. Pakistan time. Purja's device coming to rest near 5,878 m — nearly 800 vertical metres lower. Base Camp witnesses; the Karakoram Vision photograph and footage.
2. THE KEY FACT OF THE WHOLE FILM — the avalanche survival curve: about 93% survival at 15 minutes of burial, collapsing to roughly 30% by 35 minutes. Around two-thirds of avalanche deaths are asphyxia inside that window. Explain the mechanism: snow driven into the airway, rebreathed air, and the "ice mask" that forms as warm breath melts and refreezes snow across the face. Hypothermia is not the primary killer.
3. The communications failure: Base Camp lost contact shortly after 9 a.m. and saw the avalanche, yet the Baltistan Commissioner's office recorded notification at 8 p.m. and the Alpine Club of Pakistan's Naila Kiani said her body learned of it at 10 p.m. The first helicopter lifted off around 8 a.m. the next day — roughly 22.5 hours after the release.
4. THE SECOND KEY FACT: Pakistan requires climbing teams to carry helicopter evacuation insurance through Askari Aviation, written for search and rescue up to 6,000 metres. The climbers were caught at about 6,600 metres — above the ceiling of the system they were legally obliged to buy. Askari's practical safe landing altitude in the region is around 5,000 metres, roughly a vertical kilometre below the site. Helicopters were transport, not rescue.
5. The verdict this part exists to deliver: the 13 hours of silence is a genuine scandal and deserves its investigation, but even a perfect 9:35 a.m. report would have summoned an aircraft that could not reach them, to a slope where the survival window had already closed. The delay cost hope, not lives.
6. The GPS watch: Mingma David Sherpa reporting movement on Purja's tracker around 6:15 a.m., some 22 hours in, and the world briefly believing he was alive — then the device showing a recorded speed of zero. Give the three explanations for a buried tracker registering movement (the person moves, signal drift, or the avalanche deposit itself settling) and land on the last: the watch was describing a mountain shifting its weight, not a man.

PACING: Roughly 7 minutes. Slow down and hold on the 93%-to-30% survival curve — that number is the spine of the episode. Then accelerate through the institutional failures.

VISUALS: GPS track overlays and elevation profiles, a plotted survival-probability curve against burial time, a simple altitude diagram showing 6,600 m against the 6,000 m insurance ceiling and the 5,000 m landing limit, a clock-face timeline from 9:30 a.m. Thursday to 8 a.m. Friday. Respectful and clinical throughout — no recreations of burial or suffocation, no images of the deceased.

DO NOT: name or profile the individual victims — that is Part 3. Do not discuss Purja's career, records or controversies. Do not soften the conclusion.
```
